# Landing page craft process

Use this process for a new landing page, major redesign, launch page,
campaign page, or conversion-focused homepage. For a small copy or spacing
change, use only the relevant sections.

## 1. Define the decision

Write:

> When [visitor situation], [audience] needs to [understand/decide/do] so they
> can [outcome].

Then define the primary conversion, traffic intent, awareness level, business
outcome, risk, and evidence available. A page can have secondary navigation and
supporting actions, but it should not have competing primary decisions.

## 2. Gather evidence

Inspect the current route, traffic source, analytics, search intent, support
questions, sales objections, product behavior, brand system, content source,
form/provider contract, and existing page performance. Mark each claim as:

- **Observed:** directly supported by product, customer, analytics, or source
  evidence.
- **Hypothesis:** plausible but not verified.
- **Decision:** selected behavior, message, or composition and its reason.

Use references as pattern evidence. Record what is borrowed: message structure,
proof placement, density, disclosure, or interaction. Do not copy a brand's
surface style or a desktop-only pattern.

## 3. Create the message spine

Before visual design, write:

1. audience and situation;
2. outcome-led headline;
3. mechanism or differentiator;
4. primary CTA and post-click expectation;
5. strongest proof;
6. top objections and answers;
7. constraints, eligibility, pricing, privacy, or risk that must be explicit.

Test the spine without layout. If a reader cannot explain what the product is,
who it is for, why it matters, and what happens next, do not polish the page.

## 4. Choose the narrative

Select the smallest credible sequence:

Orient → Promise → Mechanism → Benefits → Prove → Resolve objections → Act

Reorder based on visitor intent. A high-intent pricing page may lead with
comparison and proof; an unfamiliar product may need context and demonstration
first. Do not add sections merely to increase scroll length.

## 5. Wireframe in content

Create a low-fidelity structure with real or clearly labelled representative
content. Check:

- first-viewport comprehension;
- section purpose and transition;
- proof near the claim it supports;
- CTA repetition at natural decision points;
- reading rhythm and information density;
- mobile order and the first useful action;
- empty, missing-media, long-copy, and failure states for interactive areas.

## 6. Compose the visual system

Define page-level tokens, type roles, content width, grid, spacing rhythm,
section rhythm, surface hierarchy, icon treatment, imagery rules, CTA states,
focus treatment, motion tokens, and responsive rules. Reuse the existing design
system where one exists. Add an exception only with a named reason.

## 7. Compose and critique in passes

Use these passes:

1. message and first viewport;
2. narrative and hierarchy;
3. content fit and proof credibility;
4. responsive composition;
5. interaction and form states;
6. accessibility and platform input;
7. accessibility, responsive behavior, performance requirements, and handoff
   dependencies;
8. subtraction and final polish.

Fix the earliest failed pass first. Do not tune shadows on a page whose offer
or CTA is unclear.

## 8. Design handoff evidence

Report the design artifact, route, target audience, primary conversion, content
source, viewport/input matrix, interactive states, screenshots/recordings
reviewed, design decisions, unresolved contract/provider dependencies, and
claims still supported only by hypothesis.
